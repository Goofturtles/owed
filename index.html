The problem

The cover is real. Finding it is the problem.

  • 90 days at most to tell your card it broke.

    Amex 30, Visa 60, Chase 90 days to report.

  • 6 years to bring a claim against the seller.

    Consumer Rights Act 2015. England, Wales and NI; five in Scotland.

What we check

Five places cover hides.

Most people know the first. The free fixes hide in the other four.

  1. 01

    The maker’s warranty

    Often still running, and some brands quietly promise a lifetime.

  2. 02

    The card you paid with

    Many cards add a free extra year, if you call within 30 to 90 days.

  3. 03

    Payouts you can still claim

    Money is set aside when a product fails. Only the people who claim see it.

  4. 04

    Quiet repair programmes

    Makers fix known faults free, without announcing it. You have to ask.

  5. 05

    The law where you live

    Your legal cover outlasts the printed warranty, sometimes by years.

Materials

What a repair actually saves.

One fixed device is a screen, a battery, a board and its copper that never have to be made twice. Nearly every rule on this page ends the same way: the part is replaced, the rest of the object stays in use.

Schematic, not to scale.

Twelve of the repair rules Owed reads. Every title is verbatim from the book.

  • California: makers must keep parts and repair manuals available for up to 7 years
  • New York: makers must hand over parts, tools and repair manuals
  • Minnesota: parts, tools and manuals within 60 days of a product’s launch
  • Oregon: makers can’t use software to block a working replacement part
  • Colorado: parts, tools and manuals for your electronics, and no software lock-outs
  • EU: makers must repair it, even after the guarantee has ended
  • EU: choosing repair adds a year to your guarantee
  • EU: phone and tablet parts must stay on sale for seven years
  • France: money knocked straight off your repair bill
  • Quebec: “we don’t make that part anymore” is no longer allowed
  • UK: the shop stays on the hook for up to six years
  • Drifting Joy-Cons are repaired free forever in the UK and Europe

On the environment, Owed counts one number: things you told it you got fixed. No CO₂ estimate, no invented multiplier.

Common questions.

Read the limitations →

Is this actually free?

Yes. Owed takes no cut of anything you claim and sells no warranties. It is a student project built to be free.

Do I need my receipt?

Not to check. Roughly when you bought it is enough. To claim you will usually need proof, and a bank statement line often counts, which is why Owed asks how you paid.

What if it finds nothing for my thing?

It says so plainly. The rules are collected by hand, so an unusual brand may come back empty. Anything on your shelf is re-checked every time you open Owed.

Is this legal advice?

No. Owed points at published rules and tells you what to ask, and every result links to its source. Strong, worth asking and long shot rate how well a rule fits your answers, not whether you qualify.

Where does my data go?

Nowhere. Your shelf and your sign-in live in your own browser’s storage. There is no server collecting what you own.

Which countries does it work in?

The US, Canada, the UK and the EU. Maker warranties lean American and Canadian, and card benefits are almost all North American. Pick your country and Owed filters to it; state rules are named by state.
