What Owed actually does

It reads the page you would skip, and marks the one line.

Owed reads the small print nobody finishes and pulls out the single clause worth asking about. For a pair of Sony headphones bought two years ago in England, that clause is the Consumer Rights Act: the shop, not the maker, stays responsible for goods that were faulty from the start, and you have six years to bring the claim (five in Scotland). It is a right to ask, not a guarantee the answer is yes.

Why it matters beyond the money

Every claimed repair is one thing that doesn’t get thrown away.

Lawmakers already did the hard part. Across the EU and a growing list of US states, makers now have to sell you the part, hand over the manual, and sometimes carry out the repair themselves — long after the warranty card says no. A rule like that only saves anything if the person holding the broken thing knows it’s there.

EU: two years of free repair or replacement, minimum New York: makers must hand over parts, tools and repair manuals UK: in the first 6 months the shop has to prove it wasn't faulty Colorado: parts, tools and manuals for your electronics US states: makers must sell you the part, the tool and the manual EU: choosing repair adds a year to your guarantee Minnesota: parts, tools and manuals within 60 days California: makers must keep parts and repair manuals for 7 years Oregon: makers can't use software to block a working part EU: makers must repair it, even after the guarantee has ended Drifting Joy-Cons are repaired free forever in the UK and Europe UK: the shop stays on the hook for up to six years

What a repair actually saves.

A claim that works keeps something you already own in service, and stops another one being built to replace it. Every screen, battery, motor and gram of copper in the thing on your shelf stays where it is. That is the whole case, and it does not need a number attached to be true.

  • Phones and tablets put on the EU market since 20 June 2025 have to still hold 80% of their charge after 800 full cycles — a battery that gives up in two years is no longer legal to sell.

    Regulation (EU) 2023/1670

  • Their spare parts have to stay on sale for seven years after the model stops being sold, and arrive within 5 to 10 working days — so "you can't get the part any more" stops being the reason a working thing gets binned.

    European Commission, ecodesign

Owed counts one number, and only one: things you told it you actually got fixed. No CO₂ estimate, no invented multiplier. A made-up figure would have looked better here and undercut the only honest thing on the page.

Questions

The fair ones first.

The ones you would ask a friend who built this.

Is this actually free?

Yes. Owed doesn't take a cut of anything you claim and doesn't sell warranties. It's a student project built to be free.

Do I need my receipt?

Not to check. Roughly when you bought it is enough to see what's likely open. You'll usually need proof when you actually claim — a bank statement line often counts, which is why Owed asks how you paid.

What if it finds nothing for my thing?

It'll say so plainly rather than invent something. That's the honest weak spot: the rules are collected by hand, so an unusual brand or model may come back empty. Adding it to your shelf means you'll hear if that changes.

Is the advice legal advice?

No. It points you at published rules and tells you what to ask. Every result links to the source so you can read it yourself, and it never claims certainty it doesn't have.

Where does my data go?

Nowhere. Your shelf and your account live in your own browser's storage. There's no server collecting what you own.

Which countries does it work in?

The maker and card rules are broad. The legal cover is strongest for the US, Canada, the UK and the EU right now — you pick your region and it only shows what applies to you.
