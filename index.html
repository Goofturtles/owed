What it does

It reads the page you skip, and marks the line.

Owed reads the small print nobody finishes and pulls out the single clause worth asking about. For a pair of Sony headphones bought two years ago in England, that clause is the Consumer Rights Act: the shop, not the maker, stays responsible for goods that were faulty from the start, and you have six years to bring the claim (five in Scotland). It is a right to ask, not a guarantee the answer is yes. Owed does not read your own documents; it matches what you own against rules it has already read.

Every claimed repair is one thing not thrown away.

Before you start

Common questions.

Is this actually free?

Yes. Owed doesn't take a cut of anything you claim and doesn't sell warranties. It's a student project built to be free.

Do I need my receipt?

Not to check — roughly when you bought it is enough. You'll usually need proof to claim, and a bank statement line often counts, which is why Owed asks how you paid.

What if it finds nothing for my thing?

It says so plainly. The rules are collected by hand, so an unusual brand may come back empty. On your shelf it is re-checked every time you open Owed.

Is the advice legal advice?

No. It points at published rules and tells you what to ask; every result links to its source. Strong / worth asking / long shot rates how well a rule fits your answers, not whether you qualify.

Where does my data go?

Nowhere. Your shelf and your account live in your own browser's storage. There's no server collecting what you own.

Which countries does it work in?

Maker warranties cover all four regions, mostly US and Canada. Card benefits are almost all North American. Consumer law: US, Canada, UK and EU. Pick your country and Owed filters to it; state rules are named by state.
